Most management books give advice. This one gives mechanics.

Steer, Don't Guess is for the owner, manager or freelancer who is drowning in work, solves problems in the order they arrive, and still cannot say whether the business is moving towards anything. The book calls this state "permanent firefighting" and replaces it with something else: steering by instruments.

The method starts from three plain questions. Where do you want to go? Where are you now, by the numbers and not by feel? What can you actually move to close the gap? Everything else in the book follows from those three.

WHAT READERS SAY

The six early reviews agree on the same points, including the critical ones.

- "Most management books give advice. This one gives mechanics." The book does not hand out tips like "delegate" or "think strategically"; it shows how to break a problem with five goals and ten constraints into parts you can handle.
- The refusal test is the idea reviewers quote most. Rank your goals by which one you would give up first and which you would defend to the end. If you don't decide that in advance, the decision will be made for you by fatigue, haste and whoever shouts loudest.
- Levers versus conditions. Reviewers call this the most liberating distinction in the book: separate what you can turn yourself (prices, the menu, terms, hours) from what you can only take into account (weather, a competitor, your own lack of resources), and stop spending energy on the second kind.
- Three reading levels in every chapter. A simple everyday example (a teenager saving for a bike), a practical case (a coffee shop, a freelance designer) and a formal level with definitions and formulas. Reviewers note that this makes the book work for a teenager planning pocket money, a small-business owner and an analyst alike.
- The author promises nothing. Every reviewer mentions it: the model is presented as a tool for thinking, not a recipe for success; forecasts can be wrong, data can be incomplete, the model does not replace industry knowledge or a financial calculation. One reviewer: "This caution makes the book's approach more credible."
- What reviewers criticise: the terminology is dense (goal vectors, state vectors, zones, control modes, three offsets), repeated examples slow the reading in places, and readers who already know goal-setting and PDCA will find part of the content familiar. The novelty, as one review puts it, is in how the ideas are arranged and applied, not in a brand-new principle.

THE METHOD

1. Goals in refusal order, each with a measure, a deadline and a place in the list.
2. Your real state, from facts, one source per number. In the book's running story, a coffee-shop owner believes he is doing fine until the numbers show that his margin is lower than he thought, because write-offs were never counted.
3. The gap between goal and state, and its zone: normal, acceptable, or red.
4. Levers you can pull, kept apart from conditions you can only account for.
5. Forecast-based control instead of looking in the rear-view mirror, and the backward move: define the end state first, then work out what must be true at each earlier stage.
6. A Red Card protocol for the moment everything is on fire and clarity is gone.

The model sits on top of methods you may already use: Balanced Scorecard, OKR, MBO, Theory of Constraints, PDCA, EOS. The book shows where each one fits.

THE AI APPENDIX, WITH A MEASUREMENT

The book includes the model's canon and a ready operating prompt for a language model. With them, an AI assistant stops giving generic advice and works with your numbers in a fixed order of analysis. This was tested: the same model answered 10 realistic cases with the canon and prompt and without them, judged blind by a stronger model against a pre-registered answer key. Score 88.9 / 100 with the method versus 55.9 without; the judge preferred the method's answer in 30 of 30 pairs; answers with an invented fact fell from 75% to 11%. The limits are stated in the book (a language-model judge, 10 cases, one model family). The book does not depend on AI: everything in it can be done on paper.

WHO IT'S FOR

Owners, managers, freelancers and anyone responsible for a result who is tired of guessing. Reviewers also recommend it for personal goals and family budgets. No background in economics is needed.

WHO IT'S NOT FOR

Anyone looking for a quick formula, a pep talk or a guaranteed result. The book asks for your participation: it works when you try it on one real problem.
