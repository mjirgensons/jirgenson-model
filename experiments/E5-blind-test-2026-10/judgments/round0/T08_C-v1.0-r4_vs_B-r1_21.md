## Step 1 — Recomputed key numbers

- Cash: 22,000 → 13,000 = −9,000 over 6 months = **−1,500/month**.
- Receivables: 8,000 → 21,000 = **+13,000** (≈ +2,167/month).
- Paper profit: 6 × 3,000 = **18,000**. Gap profit vs cash change: 18,000 − (−9,000) = **27,000**. Receivables explain 13,000 → **≈ 14,000 unexplained** (≈ 2,333/month): tax, loan repayments, equipment, drawings, or the profit figure is wrong.
- Reserve: 13,000 / 26,000 = **0.5 months** → red against a proposed 1-month line (26,000).
- Months to zero at −1,500/month: 13,000 / 1,500 ≈ **8.7 months**.
- Big customer (assumption: sales ≈ costs + profit = 29,000/month): 40% ≈ **11,600/month**; moving from 30 to 90 days ties up 2 × 11,600 ≈ **23,200**; 40% of current receivables ≈ 8,400.
- If the big customer paid nothing: other receipts ≈ 17,400 vs 26,000 out → ≈ −8,600/month → 13,000 gone in ≈ 1.5 months.
- If receivables stop growing (new normal), monthly cash flow ≈ 3,000 − 2,333 ≈ +667/month (a useful check on Response 2's forecast).

It also asserts without checking that the profit is "real".

- **M5 = 1.** The levers are a list of unpaid invoices by customer, written payment dates, asking why the customer slowed down, a deposit on big jobs, a limit on unpaid invoices before pausing new jobs, an overdraft, and 30–45-day terms for new work. "Plan works": collect €5,000–€10,000 in 4–6 weeks with new work paid in 30–45 days, giving €19,000–€25,000 in 3 months. That checks out: 13,000 + 5–10k + ≈ 2k net flow once receivables stop growing.
- **M7 = 1.** Every Monday: bank balance, total unpaid, and the big customer's unpaid amount; "Check again in 4 weeks." It lists invoices by customer with due dates today. Days-to-payment is not tracked.

### Response 2

  - F2: "the usual suspects" is hedged, with "If none fits, the profit figure itself needs checking."

## Step 4 — Criteria

### Response 1
- **C1 = 8.** Every number checks out (18,000; 27,000; 13,000/14,000; 2,300/month; 0.5 months; 11,600; 23,000; both forecasts), and the sales and forecast assumptions are labelled. One unlabelled presumption remains: "Your profit is real".
- **C2 = 7.** It correctly splits the €27,000 gap into receivables and an unexplained €14,000, and flags that the €14,000 may matter as much as the customer. It sees that the €23,000 lock-up may not be finished, and it usefully separates "their cash problem" from "new approval process". But it gives no date for the danger event and never considers that the profit figure itself may be wrong.
- **C3 = 8.** The steps are concrete and ordered by today, this week and this month, and the person can do them all. The trade-off on how hard to push is left to the owner. There are no specific dates and no escalation trigger for the big customer.
- **C4 = 9.** It answers both questions ("what's going on", "what do I do this week") in ≈ 483 plain words with one question. That fits a QUICK request well.
- **C5 = 6.** It has weekly measures, a review in 4 weeks, and the push trade-off explicitly left to the person. But the thresholds (€8,000 cash, €15,000 customer unpaid) come with no reason and are not tested against the forecast. No reserve red line is proposed, nothing is said about what to leave untouched, and no priority order is given.
- **C6 = 9.** It gives two forecasts with ranges and stated assumptions, says "No guarantee", and names the riskiest unknown (the €14,000: one-off vs repeating).

### Response 2
- **C1 = 6.** The core arithmetic is correct (27,000; half; 0.5 months; 11,600; 8,600 shortfall; end-November; 23,000/13,000; 26,000 after collection) and the assumptions are labelled ("I assume…"). Against that:
  - It invents an EU late-payment rule (€40 minimum, "most EU countries").
  - It omits the −€1,500/month rate.
  - Two small imprecisions: "€3,000 by January" ignores the ≈ +€667/month net flow, and the "around December" timing of the €10,000 alarm is loosely tied to the forecasts.
- **C2 = 9.** It names the contradiction, raises the possibility that the profit figure is wrong, and models the late payment as a step change in receivables (≈ €23,000 cap, €13,000 already locked). It names the most dangerous event with amount and date (customer stops paying, cash gone around end-November) and flags that the customer may be in trouble.
- **C3 = 9.** Every step is the owner's and has a time attached: a dated phone call with a written confirmation and a target, an accountant query, an overdraft request, the 50% upfront rule, dated escalation to full prepayment (30 October), and a cash-alarm action list (pay only wages, rent and paper; draw the overdraft).
- **C4 = 8.** It answers the question asked, stays within the budget (≈ 587 words, 3 questions) and uses everyday language. But it is near the word limit and fairly dense for a "quick analysis".
- **C5 = 9.** Every control element is present:
  - A priority order proposed with the choice left to the owner ("If your order differs, tell me"), plus an alternative if the customer matters more.
  - A red line (1 month) and an alarm (€10,000) with reasons, roughly tested against the forecast.
  - Weekly measures with a first check date and a full review date.
  - An explicit "what not to do".
  The tie of the alarm timing to the forecasts is slightly loose.
- **C6 = 9.** The forecasts are conditional and both stay "under the red line… one missed payment can finish it". It states its assumptions and names the riskiest one (that most of the rise is this customer), and asks about it. It makes no promises. The robustness claim ("a 10% error… does not change the conclusion") is unverified but plausible.

It has a dated collection call with a target, a proposed red line the owner is already below, a dated escalation for the big customer, a cash-alarm action list, a priority order and a "what not to do".
