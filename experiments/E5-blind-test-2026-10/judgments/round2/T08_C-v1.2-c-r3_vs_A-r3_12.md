# Judgment — T08 (pair C-v1.2-c-r3 vs A-r3, order 12)

## Step 1. Recomputed key numbers

- Cash: 22,000 → 13,000 = −9,000 over 6 months = **−1,500/month**.
- Receivables: 8,000 → 21,000 = **+13,000** (≈ +2,167/month).
- Paper profit: 6 × 3,000 = 18,000. 18,000 − 13,000 = 5,000 should have come into the bank. Cash actually fell 9,000, so ≈ **14,000 is unexplained** (≈ 2,333/month). Possible causes include tax/VAT, loan principal, equipment, owner drawings, or a wrong profit figure. It is a question, not a known cause.
- Reserve: 13,000 / 26,000 = **0.5 months** (≈ 2 weeks). A 1-month red line would be 26,000, so the shop is already below it.
- Months to zero at −1,500/month: 13,000 / 1,500 ≈ **8.7 months**.
- Implied monthly sales ≈ 26,000 + 3,000 = 29,000 (an assumption). 40% ≈ 11,600/month; other customers ≈ 17,400/month. If the big customer paid nothing: −8,600/month, so 13,000 / 8,600 ≈ **1.5 months**. The big customer's likely share of receivables is ≈ 40% × 21,000 ≈ 8,400.
- 60 extra days of the big customer's sales ≈ 2 × 11,600 = 23,200. That is more than the observed +13,000, so the slip is recent or partial and receivables may keep rising.
- Dates: 17 Oct 2026 is a Saturday, so Mon 19 Oct, Thu 22 Oct, Fri 23 Oct and Mon 16 Nov are all correct weekdays.
- "Plan works" check for Response 1: receivables stop growing, so the monthly change is about −1,500 + 2,167 ≈ +667. Add 6,500 recovered (half of 13,000). Over about 2.5 months to end-December that gives ≈ 13,000 + 6,500 + 1,700 ≈ 21,200 at the top. The lower end (~15,000) fits if the burn continues. The range €15,000–21,000 is consistent.
- "Nothing changes" check for Response 1: 13,000 − (2.3 to 3.3 months × 1,500) ≈ 8,000–9,500 by end-December. Consistent.

It then treats the cash reserve as the alarm ("below it one bad month stops wages… alarm already ringing"). Cash is clearly the number that governs, though the response never literally says "cash governs".
- **M2 = 1.** "Your €13,000 is half a month of expenses… I propose a red line of one month of expenses, because below it one bad month stops wages. You are already under it."
- **M3 = 1.** It states −€1,500/month (in the forecast) and +€13,000 of receivables. It notes the €14,000 (€2,300/month) and asks about it in Q1 ("what did you take out or pay over six months…?").
- **M4 = 1.** "If the big customer paid nothing, your €13,000 would last about 1.5 months, to around the start of December, and wages would be hit first." This gives the amount at stake as the monthly flow (€11,600, leaving a hole of €8,600). It does not give the receivable they owe, but asks for it in Q2. The effect on time-to-zero is stated.
- **M5 = 1.** The levers are a written request for a payment date plus half of the overdue amount, checking contract terms and late interest, ageing and chasing everything over 30 days, deposits or staged invoices on new big jobs, and deposits only after the trigger date. Credit limit, shorter terms and factoring are missing, but the levers are clearly on collection and terms.
- **M6 = 1.** Nothing changes: −1,500/month, giving €8,000–9,500 at end-December (months-to-zero is not stated, but the numbers are correct). Plan works: half of the extra €13,000 comes back and receivables stop growing, giving €15,000–21,000.
- **M7 = 1.** "Every Monday: bank balance, total unpaid invoices and the part older than 30 days, and what the big customer owes. First check 19 October, full review 16 November." Days to payment is not explicit, but ageing and the big customer's balance are covered.
- **M8 = 0.** Three questions is within the limit, but the response is about 614 words, over the 600-word budget.

- **M2 = 0.** It computes the reserve ("about two weeks" ≈ 0.5 months) but proposes no red line.
- **M3 = 0.** It gives the burn (about €1,500/month), +€13,000 and the €14,000 gap. But it never asks the person about the gap; it only hands it to the accountant. The element requires it to be asked.
- **M4 = 0.** "If you learn they're in financial trouble, treat the overdue amount as a possible loss" and "receivables could also keep climbing" mention the event. There is no amount at risk and no effect on time-to-zero.
- **M5 = 1.** Phone the customer, part-payment, a deposit on new work until overdue is cleared, chase all overdue invoices, 30–50% deposits, 14-day terms, a credit limit per customer, and late-payment interest. These are strong collection and terms levers; factoring is not mentioned.
- **M6 = 0.** There is no forecast. It only recommends building a 13-week forecast.
- **M7 = 0.** There is no weekly measurement routine and no review date. The 13-week forecast is a planning tool, not a check routine.
- **M8 = 1.** Zero questions and about 482 words.

## Step 3. Forbidden items and invented facts

### Response 1
- **F1:** no. It explicitly rules out equipment, hiring and stock now.
- **F2:** no, but borderline. "€14,000… went out for things that are not in the profit figure: your withdrawals, loan repayments, equipment, tax or VAT, stock" lists candidate categories, not a single cause. The response then asks (Q1) and says "Do not borrow until you know where the €14,000 went." Slight overreach: it excludes the possibility that the profit figure itself is wrong, and "That explains the €14,000" is assertive about the bridge.
- **F3:** no (3 questions).
- **F4:** no. The forecasts are conditional ranges.
- **Invented facts:** none. Sales of ~€29,000 is labelled ("taking sales as"), steady profit is labelled, and "90 days = after invoice" is labelled. One unlabelled simplification: the "paid nothing" scenario assumes other customers pay on time and ignores the unexplained outflow. That is an implicit assumption, not an invented fact.

### Response 2
- **F1:** no. "Reduce dependence on one customer" is a next-month item, not growth or marketing as the first move.
- **F2:** no. The causes are framed as "the usual suspects", and the response defers to a reconciliation. Like Response 1, it assumes the gap is a non-P&L outflow and not a profit-figure error.
- **F3:** no (0 questions).
- **F4:** no.
- **Invented facts:**
  - "In the EU, businesses can usually claim interest and a fixed recovery fee on late B2B payments." This rule is not in the task. It is hedged ("check your country's rules") and substantively accurate.
  - "[An overdraft] is much easier to get while your account still looks healthy." This is a general claim presented as fact, and the account at 0.5 months of cover is arguably not healthy.
  - Minor: "Depreciation makes the gap slightly bigger" assumes depreciation exists. The mechanism is correct.

## Step 4. Criteria

### Response 1
- **C1 = 8.** All arithmetic checks out (5,000; 14,000; 2,300/month; 0.5 months; 11,600/17,400/8,600; 1.5 months; 23,200; both forecast ranges), and the main assumptions are labelled. It loses points for the mildly assertive "went out for things not in the profit figure" and for an unlabelled simplification in the default scenario.
- **C2 = 9.** It finds the real issue (paper profit vs cash, plus a €14,000 hole the late customer does not explain). It names the most dangerous event with a date and the damage (cash gone around early December, wages first). It also spots that the data do not fully fit (the slip is recent, so receivables may keep rising).
- **C3 = 9.** Each step has an owner and a date (Mon 19, Thu 22, Fri 23 October, "this month"). Fast, reversible moves come first, and the triggers have dates and thresholds. A credit limit and shorter terms are missing.
- **C4 = 7.** It answers exactly what was asked, in plain English, with 3 questions within budget. It has one breach: about 614 words against a 600-word limit, a small overrun but still over.
- **C5 = 9.** It proposes a priority order and leaves the choice to the person ("If your order differs, tell me"). The red line has a reason and is tested against the forecast ("Reaching the €26,000 line takes longer"). It sets weekly Monday checks, first check and review dates, triggers at a date and at €6,000, and a clear "Not now" list. Days-to-payment is not tracked.
- **C6 = 9.** It gives two numeric forecasts as conditional ranges, labels its assumptions, says openly that the figures do not fully fit, names what would narrow the ranges, and makes no promises.

### Response 2
- **C1 = 7.** The bridge, the €2,300/month and the two weeks of cover are correct. Few assumptions are labelled beyond the "rounded numbers" caveat. A legal rule and a general claim about bank lending appear as fact; they are hedged or generic but not from the task.
- **C2 = 6.** The bridge and the €14,000 gap are well diagnosed, and it rightly says the average burn hides timing risk. But it names no dated danger event, no amount at risk and no time-to-zero, so the nearest irreversible damage stays vague.
- **C3 = 7.** Six concrete, correct steps for this week and good next-month terms changes (deposits, 14-day terms, credit limits, late interest). The steps have no owners or dates beyond "this week" or "next month".
- **C4 = 8.** About 482 words, no questions, English, and it answers the question. Some jargon ("P&L", "B2B", "principal repayments", "reconciliation") is a slight mismatch for a small-shop owner.
- **C5 = 4.** "Hold what can wait" covers what to leave untouched, and per-customer credit limits are a kind of threshold. There is no priority order, no red line, no measurement routine and no review date.
- **C6 = 5.** It is cautious and promises nothing ("treat as a possible loss", "rounded numbers"). It gives no forecasts or ranges, although this mode calls for two.
